Chapter One

The Alarm, the Cat, and the Catastrophe

The alarm did not ring so much as it detonated. At 6:47 on a Monday morning in Hyderabad, Maya Rao's phone launched itself into a rendition of a cheerful pop song so aggressively upbeat it could have been used as a weapon. Mr. Biscuit, her three-year-old tabby and full-time antagonist, took one look at the vibrating device, nudged it off the nightstand, and returned to sleep with the satisfied expression of someone who has completed their life's work.

Maya lay there for eleven seconds — she counted — staring at the ceiling fan's lazy revolution, negotiating silently with the universe. The universe, as it does every Monday, declined to negotiate.

By the time she reached the kitchen, she had already lost one earring, discovered her favourite kurta had a mysterious new stain on the collar, and learned that Mr. Biscuit had, at some point in the night, knocked her carefully prepared Monday bag entirely off the hook. Her laptop charger was now under the sofa. Her headphones were in the bathroom. Her keys were — she would find out later — inside her own shoe.

"Monday does not sneak up on you.
It arrives — loudly, thoroughly, without apology."

Chapter Two

The Coffee That Saved Everything

There is a particular quality to the silence of a kitchen at 7:15 AM when everything has already gone wrong and the only remaining hope is coffee. Maya stood before her moka pot with the focused reverence of a person performing a sacred ritual, which is exactly what it was.

She measured the grounds. She filled the base with water — cold, filtered, precisely to the valve line. She assembled the pot with the care of someone who has learned, the hard way, what happens when you don't tighten it properly. She placed it on the flame and stepped back.

The kitchen filled with that smell — dark and warm and utterly reliable — the one thing in her morning that had not betrayed her. When she poured it into her chipped ceramic mug — the blue one that said WORLD'S OKAYEST HUMAN, a gift from Dev that she pretended to find annoying and secretly loved — she felt the particular peace of the chemically caffeinated. The chaos was still there. But now she could look at it.

Chapter Three

The Mashup Begins

The auto she flagged down had a driver who was playing a mashup of 90s Telugu film songs remixed into lo-fi beats. Normally Maya would have found this irritating. Today, with the coffee warm in her stomach and the morning sun slanting gold across Hyderabad's chaotic streets, it sounded like the soundtrack to a life she was actively enjoying.

Dev was waiting outside her office building, holding two croissants and wearing an expression of someone who knows exactly how absurd everything is and has made peace with it. "You look," he said, handing her a croissant, "like a person who lost her keys in her own shoe." She stared. "Mr. Biscuit texted me," he said simply, and she chose to believe him.

Upstairs, at her desk, she opened her laptop to find 247 unread emails. She scrolled past forty-three newsletters, eleven meeting invites, seven passive-aggressive threads about the office kitchen, and then — there, near the bottom, from an address she almost didn't recognise — an email from a design studio in Berlin. They had seen her portfolio. They had a project. They wanted to talk.

"The best things arrive on
Mondays — disguised as ordinary days."

Chapter Four

The Mashup, Resolved

She read the email three times. Then she walked to Dev's desk and showed him the screen without saying anything. He read it, looked up at her, and said — very quietly, for Dev — "Maya. This is it." And he was right. It was it.

Later, she would try to explain what made that Monday different from all the other Mondays. It wasn't the chaos, which was ordinary. It wasn't the coffee, though the coffee helped. It was something about the way the whole day had been a kind of mashup — bad alarm remixed with good light, lost keys remixed with found opportunity, Mr. Good Day's sabotage remixed with Dev's inexplicable croissants — all of it layered together into something that felt, improbably, like music.

She replied to the Berlin studio. She CC'd nobody. She pressed send at 9:33 AM on a Monday, and outside her window, Hyderabad continued being itself — loud and golden and magnificent and late — and everything, for one beautiful moment, was exactly as it should be.

Mr. Good Day, at home, knocked something else off a shelf. He would not apologise. He never did. That, too, felt right.

"Monday is not the enemy. Monday is the opening act. The rest of the week is yours."
